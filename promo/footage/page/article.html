The Islands That Build Themselves

Atolls look like accidents of scenery. They are, in fact, the slow work of corals, algae, storms and a great many parrotfish.

By Mara Ellison•November 2026•14 min read

Fly over the central Pacific and the islands appear without warning: thin crescents of white sand and green palms, strung around lagoons so still and turquoise they look painted on. There is no mountain behind them, no river delta, no obvious reason for land to be there at all. The seafloor around them can be four or five kilometres down, yet here is a beach you can walk across in a few minutes.

These are reef islands, and almost everything about them is made by living things. The rock under your feet is the compacted skeletons of corals and algae. The sand is ground-up shell, urchin spine and coral that has passed through the gut of a fish. Even the shape of the island is set by the reef around it, which breaks the swell and decides where sediment can settle.

Understanding how such places form took more than a century, two drilling campaigns and one very patient naturalist. It also matters a great deal today, because the answer tells us whether these islands, home to more than half a million people, can keep pace with a rising sea.

Darwin’s sinking mountains

When Charles Darwin sailed into the Cocos (Keeling) Islands aboard HMS Beagle in April 1836, the leading explanation for atolls was that corals had colonised the rims of submerged volcanic craters. Darwin found that hard to believe. Craters do not come in the sizes and shapes he was seeing, and reef-building corals cannot live more than a few dozen metres below the surface, where sunlight fades.

His alternative was bold and, at the time, impossible to test. Start with a volcanic island ringed by a fringing reef. As the extinct volcano slowly sinks under its own weight, the corals keep growing upward toward the light. The reef becomes a barrier reef, separated from the shrinking island by a lagoon. When the last peak disappears, only the ring of reef remains: an atoll.

Darwin published the idea in 1842, in The Structure and Distribution of Coral Reefs. It was elegant and it explained the geography, but it made a specific prediction that nobody could check: if he was right, a hole drilled through an atoll should pass through a thick cap of reef limestone before striking volcanic rock.

“We feel a surprise when travellers tell us of the vast dimensions of the Pyramids and other great ruins, but how utterly insignificant are the greatest of these, when compared to these mountains of stone accumulated by the agency of various minute and tender animals!”

Charles Darwin, Journal of Researches

The Royal Society tried in the 1890s, sending expeditions to Funafuti, in what is now Tuvalu. The deepest bore reached about 340 metres and brought up nothing but coral rock. It was suggestive, but not conclusive. The question stayed open until 1952, when geologists working on Enewetak Atoll drilled two deep holes. Both passed through more than 1,200 metres of limestone before hitting basalt. Darwin had been right, more than a century after he said it.

A living limestone factory

The engine behind all of this is a partnership. Reef-building corals are small animals, polyps a few millimetres across, but inside their tissues live single-celled algae that photosynthesise and hand most of the sugar they make to their host. That energy lets the coral pull calcium and carbonate out of seawater and lay down a skeleton of aragonite, the same mineral as mother-of-pearl.

Each polyp adds only a thin layer at a time. A massive brain coral may grow a centimetre a year; branching corals can manage ten times that. Spread across a reef, though, the numbers add up. A healthy reef can produce several kilograms of limestone per square metre every year, enough to keep pace with all but the fastest changes in sea level.

Corals do not work alone. Crustose coralline algae, pink and rock-hard crusts that look more like paint than plants, grow over loose fragments and cement them into a solid framework. On the windward edge of many Pacific atolls they build a raised algal ridge that takes the full force of the surf. Without that glue, a reef would be a heap of rubble.

A reef that can build an island needs a few things at once:

  • A shallow reef flat, close enough to the surface to trap sediment
  • A steady supply of sand and gravel from the living reef
  • Storms strong enough to move that material, but rare enough to let it settle
  • Time: most of today’s reef islands are only a few thousand years old

Where the sand comes from

Stand on an atoll beach and pick up a handful of sand. Under a hand lens it is a museum of the reef: broken coral branches, fragments of mollusc shell, the spines of sea urchins, the tiny chambered shells of foraminifera, and flakes of the green alga Halimeda, which grows as chains of calcified discs.

A surprising amount of it has been eaten. Parrotfish graze on algae growing over dead coral, scraping the surface with fused, beak-like teeth and grinding what they bite in their throats. What they cannot digest they excrete as fine white sediment. A single large parrotfish can turn out hundreds of kilograms of sand a year.

Researchers who trace sand back to its sources find that the mix varies from island to island. In parts of the Maldives, parrotfish dominate; elsewhere, foraminifera or Halimeda do much of the work. But the principle holds everywhere: the beach is only as healthy as the reef that feeds it. When corals die and fish disappear, the supply line slowly runs dry.

Built by storms

Sand alone does not make dry land. On a calm day, waves move sediment back and forth across the reef flat without lifting it above the tide. What finally builds an island is violence: cyclones and large swells that tear blocks of coral from the reef edge and hurl gravel and boulders onto the flat.

Those storm deposits form ridges of rubble, and the ridges become anchors. Finer sand piles up in their lee, plants take hold, and roots and fallen leaves begin to bind the surface. Over centuries a low island takes shape, rarely more than three or four metres above high tide. Across Polynesia these islets are called motu.

Most of today’s reef islands are surprisingly young. Across much of the Pacific, sea level stood a metre or two higher than it does now some four to five thousand years ago, then slowly fell. As it dropped, reef flats that had been under water came within reach of waves and storms, and islands began to accumulate on top of them.

Islands that move

Because they are made of loose sediment, reef islands are not fixed in place. They behave more like sandbars than like mountains. Shorelines advance in one season and retreat in the next; sand migrates from the ocean side to the lagoon side; whole islets can shift tens of metres in a decade.

That mobility is easy to mistake for decline. A photograph of a single eroding beach makes a dramatic image, but the sand has often moved somewhere else rather than vanished. When geographers compared historical aerial photographs with recent satellite imagery, the picture was more mixed than many had expected.

A 2018 study of 101 islands in Tuvalu found that nearly three-quarters had grown in area over four decades, even as the local sea level rose at about twice the global average. Total land area across the country increased by almost three percent. Some islands shrank, a few disappeared, and others appeared or merged.

None of this means the islands are safe. It means they are alive, in a geological sense: they respond to waves, storms and sediment supply in ways that a simple flooding model, which treats them like blocks of concrete, does not capture.

An uncertain tide

The same processes that built reef islands could let some of them keep up with a rising ocean, but only if the reef keeps producing sediment. That is where the real worry lies. Marine heatwaves now trigger mass coral bleaching every few years, and a bleached reef that dies stops making new limestone and sand.

There is also a human dimension. Seawalls built to protect a village can lock an island in place, stopping it from adjusting to storms and sometimes starving neighbouring beaches of sand. Engineers in the Maldives and Kiribati are experimenting with approaches that work with sediment rather than against it, from beach nourishment to raising land on purpose.

For the people who live on atolls, none of this is abstract. Freshwater lenses beneath the islands turn brackish after storm surges, and every reef that dies takes food off the table. The future of these islands depends on emissions decided far away, and on how well the reefs that make them are looked after.

Darwin showed that an atoll marks the grave of a vanished mountain. It is also something more hopeful: proof that a reef, given time and a little calm water, can raise solid ground from the open ocean. Whether it gets that time is now partly up to us.
